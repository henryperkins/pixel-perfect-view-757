## Item 01 — Ready

- **Recognition — 4.** “‘I can do this much’ may feel like too little enthusiasm for an idea you care about.” The conditional project situation is easy to locate in life, and the quoted phrase gives the reader a particular moment rather than an abstract capacity problem.
- **Meaning — 4.** “promising more can become a way of trying to show how much the project matters” identifies a reason for overcommitting without claiming it is the reader's motive. This explains why a limit may be difficult to express instead of merely saying that limits matter.
- **Useful direction — 4.** “you could describe both what you want to make possible and the part you can take on now” offers one bounded response to the exact tension. The first-stage example makes the response available today without requiring a larger commitment.
- **Distinctness — 4.** “Enthusiasm and a commitment of capacity can be expressed separately.” The phrasing is stiff, but the distinction is specific: the size of a promise need not carry all the evidence of one's interest. This is now more than general advice about scope and structure.
- **Voice/readability — 4.** “‘I'd love to help bring this to life, and I can take on the first stage’” sounds like something someone could say. The plain example anchors the conceptual middle paragraph, although the final explanatory sentence is unnecessary.

**Remaining reader defects, not blocking:** “a commitment of capacity” is more administrative than ordinary speech; simplify that phrase while preserving the separation between enthusiasm and what is being promised. “That describes a commitment to one stage without promising the rest” explains an already clear example and can be cut. The reflection is earned: it returns to seriousness at a manageable scale without assigning a second task. The central distinctness defect in the initial version is resolved.

Recheck the resulting rhythm rather than treating deletion alone as automatic qualification.
